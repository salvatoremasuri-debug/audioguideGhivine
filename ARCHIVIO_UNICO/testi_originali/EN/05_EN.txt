5. THE HALL OF ORGAN PIPES

We are now inside the Hall of Organ Pipes, one of the most spectacular settings on the entire route through the Grotta del Bue Marino.

This is where the most imposing and impressive formations are concentrated. The hall is characterised mainly by four large columns, among the oldest formations visible on the tourist route: their age is estimated at around 500,000 years.

But how do these structures form?
It all begins at the surface, with rain.
Rainwater, as it seeps into the mountain, passes through the soil and becomes enriched with carbon dioxide. When water and carbon dioxide combine, a slightly acidic solution forms, capable of eroding the limestone.

As the water works its way inside the mountain, it dissolves the calcium carbonate, the constituent element of the rock, transforming it into calcium bicarbonate, a soluble form that is carried along in solution.

When this mineral-rich water reaches the underground cavities, such as this hall, the process changes.
If a drop remains suspended from the ceiling, it loses carbon dioxide and slowly deposits calcium carbonate, which crystallises and solidifies to form a stalactite.
If instead the drop falls to the ground, the mineral is deposited on the floor, giving rise to a stalagmite.
When, over the course of thousands of years, a stalactite and a stalagmite meet and join together, they form a column, like those we see here.

These are extremely slow processes.
It can take approximately 100 years to form just one cubic centimetre of formation.
This means that every structure we see is the result of immensely long geological timeframes and a constant balance between water, rock, and atmosphere.

In this hall, we can read, almost like in a natural archive, the invisible journey of water through the heart of the mountain.
Every formation is the concrete trace of that silent journey, repeated drop by drop for hundreds of thousands of years.
