3. THE HALL OF CANDELABRAS

Welcome to the evocative Hall of Candelabras, so named because the speleothems found in this section of the route resemble, in shape and colour, elegant nineteenth-century candelabras, with wax seemingly dripping slowly from the candles.

We are now in the first of the four halls we will visit along a route of approximately one kilometre.
In reality, however, the Grotta del Bue Marino is part of a much larger karst system.
The cave you are visiting today connects, through underground galleries, to three other cavities: the Grotta di Su Palu, the Grotta di Su Molente, and the Grotta di Monte Longos. There are therefore four external entrances leading to a single vast underground system, with approximately 75 kilometres explored so far. It is important to stress this: the system is still being explored. We do not yet know its true extent. With its 75 kilometres already documented, it is currently the second longest karst system in Italy.

The Grotta del Bue Marino is the only part of the system accessible to the public and is divided into three branches:
the northern branch, approximately 9 km long, currently closed to the public;
the central branch, 5 km long, accessible only to cave divers;
the southern branch, 8 km long, the one we are visiting today, of which only the first kilometre has been open to visitors since 1983, the year of its first public opening.

The southern branch is still active.
Along the route you will notice dripping water, a sign that the formations are still slowly developing: the cave is constantly changing, even if imperceptibly to our eyes.

But the cave also changes through another, far more powerful force: the erosive action of an underground river that still flows today and continues to carve out these galleries.
For almost the entire length of our route, there is seawater beneath our feet.
At the end of the route, we will instead encounter an underground river: ahead of us will flow fresh, clear, and silent water.

It is precisely this watercourse that is the principal architect of the entire karst system. Through its erosive force, exerted over millions of years, it has carved through the limestone, shaping galleries, widening cavities, and contributing to the formation of most of the 75 kilometres explored to date.

As you walk through these halls, try to imagine a time when the spaces you see were completely submerged. During the early stages of its formation, the cave was filled with fresh water right up to the ceiling. The mighty underground river occupied every space, exerting a continuous erosive action on the rock in its slow and ceaseless journey in search of a natural outlet.

That outlet was found at the sea: the current entrance to the Grotta del Bue Marino coincides in fact with the mouth of that underground river, the terminal stretch of a vast system shaped by water over geological time.

When the river opened its outlet to the sea, the water level inside the cave gradually dropped. The halls emptied and the sea became part of the underground environment.
Only then, drop by drop, in silence and darkness, did all the formations you see today begin to slowly grow and take shape.

What we are observing is not simply a cave, but the result of a long and uninterrupted dialogue between water and stone: a natural work of art shaped by the silent force of a river hidden in the heart of the mountain.
